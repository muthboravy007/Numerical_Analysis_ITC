## Example 8.1 — Zipf's law: fitting a power law

**Problem.** Word frequencies follow roughly $f(r) = br^{a}$ in the rank $r$. Given 50 noisy frequencies generated with $b = 0.08$ and $a = -1.05$ (multiplicative noise), estimate $(b,a)$.

**Solution.** Linearising gives $\ln f = \ln b+a\ln r$. A least-squares line in log–log coordinates yields $\hat b = 0.0767$ and $\hat a = -1.032$. Nonlinear least squares on the raw scale yields $0.0808$ and $-1.053$.

| method | SSE on the raw scale |
|---|---|
| log–log line | $2.7\times10^{-5}$ |
| nonlinear least squares | $6.8\times10^{-6}$ |

**Take-away.** The two fits minimise different criteria. The log–log fit weights all ranks equally in *relative* error, which is statistically appropriate for multiplicative noise and is what we generated. The raw-scale fit is dominated by the top few words. Choose the loss that matches the noise model, not the one that is easiest to compute.

## Example 8.2 — A cheap polynomial sigmoid

**Problem.** Embedded devices sometimes replace $\sigma(x) = 1/(1+e^{-x})$ by a polynomial. Find the degree needed for a maximum error of about $10^{-5}$ on $[-6,6]$.

**Solution.** Interpolate at Chebyshev points (via `chebinterpolate`):

| degree | 3 | 5 | 9 | 15 | 21 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| max error | $8.2\times10^{-2}$ | $3.1\times10^{-2}$ | $4.2\times10^{-3}$ | $2.1\times10^{-4}$ | $1.0\times10^{-5}$ |

The degree-5 *Taylor* polynomial about 0 has maximum error **12.7** on the same interval.

**Take-away.** Taylor polynomials are excellent near one point and useless far from it. Chebyshev approximations spread the error evenly (near-minimax). The convergence is geometric but slow, because $\sigma$ has poles at $\pm i\pi$, close to the interval once it is scaled to $[-1,1]$.

## Example 8.3 — Orthogonal polynomials on data points

**Problem.** Fit a cubic to $y = e^x\cos3x$ at 11 equally spaced points on $[0,1]$, using polynomials orthogonal *with respect to the data points*, generated by the three-term recurrence.

**Solution.** With $\phi_0 = 1$, $\phi_1 = x-\bar x$ and $\phi_{k+1} = (x-B_k)\phi_k-C_k\phi_{k-1}$ (sums over the data points), the Gram matrix is diagonal: $\operatorname{diag}(11, 1.1, 0.0858, 0.00618)$, with off-diagonal entries of order $10^{-16}$. Each coefficient is $a_k = \frac{\sum y\phi_k}{\sum\phi_k^2}$, giving $(-0.3115, -4.0170, -3.8439, 4.5205)$. The fit is identical to monomial least squares. Dropping the cubic term leaves the first three coefficients unchanged.

**Take-away.** This is Forsythe's method, and the same idea is behind QR. It avoids the ill-conditioned monomial normal equations ($\kappa = 10^4$ here, and exponentially worse at higher degree). Adding a degree does not change the earlier coefficients, which makes forward selection of the degree cheap.

## Example 8.4 — Padé beats Taylor far from the expansion point

**Problem.** Compare the $[2/2]$ Padé approximant of $\ln(1+x)$ with its degree-4 Taylor polynomial.

**Solution.** $r(x) = \dfrac{x+\frac12x^2}{1+x+\frac16x^2}$.

| $x$ | $\ln(1+x)$ | Padé error | Taylor-4 error |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0.5 | 0.405465 | $6\times10^{-5}$ | $4.4\times10^{-3}$ |
| 1 | 0.693147 | $8.4\times10^{-4}$ | 0.11 |
| 2 | 1.098612 | $7.7\times10^{-3}$ | 2.4 |
| 4 | 1.609438 | $4.4\times10^{-2}$ | 48 |

**Take-away.** The Taylor series of $\ln(1+x)$ has radius of convergence 1, so it diverges for $x>1$. The rational approximation, built from the *same five coefficients*, mimics the logarithmic growth and remains usable at $x = 4$.

## Example 8.5 — Seasonality in daily temperatures

**Problem.** A year of daily temperatures is simulated with mean 28, an annual cycle of amplitude 3 peaking on day 110, a semi-annual cycle of amplitude 1, and noise with SD 0.8. Recover the seasonal parameters by trigonometric least squares.

**Solution.** The regressors are $1, \cos\theta, \sin\theta, \cos2\theta, \sin2\theta$ with $\theta = 2\pi d/365$. Over a full cycle they are exactly orthogonal: $X^TX/365 = \operatorname{diag}(1, \frac12, \frac12, \frac12, \frac12)$, with off-diagonal entries $0$. The estimates are:
- mean $27.95$;
- annual amplitude $\sqrt{a_1^2+b_1^2} = 3.01$;
- peak day $\frac{365}{2\pi}\operatorname{atan2}(b_1,a_1) = 108.9$;
- semi-annual amplitude $0.99$.

**Take-away.** The amplitude/phase form turns regression coefficients into interpretable quantities, such as "when is the peak". Complete cycles make the normal equations diagonal, just as the DFT's orthogonality does.

## Example 8.6 — Convolution by FFT

**Problem.** Smooth a signal of length $n$ with a moving average of width $k$. Compare direct convolution ($O(nk)$) with FFT convolution ($O(n\log n)$).

**Solution.**

| $n$ | $k$ | direct | FFT |
|---|---|---|---|
| $10^5$ | 101 | 0.003 s | 0.014 s |
| $10^5$ | 10 001 | 0.38 s | **0.011 s** |
| $10^6$ | 10 001 | 3.1 s | **0.19 s** |

The two results agree to $10^{-16}$.

**Take-away.** For short kernels, direct convolution (highly optimised) wins. For long kernels the FFT wins by orders of magnitude. The convolution theorem underlies fast filtering, kernel density estimation on grids, and polynomial and big-integer multiplication.

## Example 8.7 — Spectral leakage and windowing

**Problem.** A 4-second recording at 100 Hz contains a strong 10.1 Hz tone and a weak 14.3 Hz tone, 40 dB weaker. Can the periodogram reveal the weak tone?

**Solution.** The frequency grid has spacing $1/T = 0.25$ Hz, so 10.1 Hz falls *between* bins and its energy leaks everywhere.

| window | power near 14.25 Hz | leakage level at 20 Hz | weak tone visible? |
|---|---|---|---|
| rectangular | −36.8 dB | −40.1 dB | **no** (buried in leakage) |
| Hann | −39.3 dB | −104.6 dB | **yes** |

**Take-away.** A finite record multiplies the signal by a rectangle, whose transform decays only like $1/f$. Tapering (Hann) lowers the sidelobes by about 60 dB, at the price of a slightly wider main lobe. Always window before looking for weak periodicities, e.g. in sales, sensor or climate data.
